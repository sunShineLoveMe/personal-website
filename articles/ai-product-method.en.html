Product Method for the AI Era:
Designing a Product That Fails, and Validating It

When output is a distribution of quality rather than a fixed value, requirements, design, prototyping and acceptance all have to change.

Author: ChrisAbout 20 min read6 figures · 1 table · 1 checklist

ABSTRACT

What fundamentally separates an AI feature from a traditional one is that its output is not a point but a quality distribution. Starting from that, this article proposes a working framework for product managers and developers:

  1. three screening questions before kickoff, and a “cost × detectability” matrix;
  2. a five-level autonomy dial, and designing for “after the error”;
  3. a six-rung prototype validation ladder from storyboard to gray release, each rung answering one question;
  4. treating the eval set as an executable requirements document, and expressing cost and latency as product attributes.

It closes with how people and AI divide the work in a real project (Keysuit), and the limitations of this method.

01First, admit this: you are designing something that makes mistakes

A traditional software spec reads “given input X, produce output Y.” Click “Export” and you get either a PDF or an error message. The behavior can be enumerated, asserted, and accepted in one pass.

AI features are not like that. For the same input, a model may produce ten outputs of different quality: some impressive, some mediocre, and occasionally one that is plainly off. What you ship is not a point but a quality distribution. The real user experience is determined by the shape of that distribution, especially the tail on the left.

Three consequences follow, and they are the starting point of every method in the rest of this article:

  • Demos systematically overstate the product. In a demo people pick the example that ran best, which sits at the right end of the curve. Users sample the whole curve in real use, and remember the worst few draws.
  • “Done right” has to be redefined. A requirement such as “the system shall extract all clauses correctly” is no longer verifiable. It must become “on this class of samples, what share of outputs reaches the acceptable line.”
  • There is one more layer to design: failure. You are designing not only the interface for when things work, but also whether users can notice a failure, whether they can correct it, and what it costs them.
DEMO TRAP

An idea approved only because “I tried it a few times and it worked great” proves nothing more than that the right end of the curve exists. It does not prove the left end is affordable.

02Ask whether AI is worth using before asking how

Most failed AI products did not fail because the model was too weak; they chose the wrong problem at the start. Before kicking off a project I run three questions. If any one of them has no answer, I hold off.

Question 1: How much does the dumbest non-AI approach already solve?

First write a baseline built from rules, templates or retrieval. It is worth two things: a comparison, because without a baseline you cannot tell what the model contributes; and, often, the baseline is already good enough, which saves you the whole apparatus of evals, cost and maintenance that follows. Anthropic’s first piece of advice, in summarizing what it learned about building agents, follows a similar line: start with the simplest solution and add complexity only when results truly fall short.[2]

Question 2: How costly is an error, and is it easy to notice?

This is the figure in this article I value most. An AI feature can be placed in a matrix of “cost of an error” against “how hard the error is to notice,” and its position determines how much autonomy you can give it.

The key is the top-right corner: high cost, and errors that are hard to notice. If a compliance check misses a risk, nobody notices at the time, but the cost afterward can be huge. A feature like that should not be packaged as “completes automatically.” Narrow its scope and back it with strong evals, or do not build it yet. At the other extreme, in the bottom-left, polishing and summarization produce errors the user can see at a glance; let it run, and let adoption rate do the talking.

Question 3: Can we tell whether it got better or worse?

Without an eval suite that can be run repeatedly, every prompt change and every model swap is tuning blindfolded. Section 5 covers this in detail. The conclusion first: a feature whose evaluation plan you cannot write has probably not been thought through to the problem it solves.

03Design: treat uncertainty as a design material

When output is unpredictable, interface and flow become part of product quality. I group the design decisions into four kinds.

3.1 Autonomy is a dial, not a switch

“Should it execute automatically?” should not be a yes-or-no question. Break it into five levels; each step up demands stronger evidence.

A common mistake is to jump straight from L1 to L5 because the demo looks good. A steadier path is to launch at a low level, use real adoption data to prove that a class of tasks can move up, and release class by class. Within one product, different tasks can sit at different levels.

3.2 Draw “after the error” into the main flow

Every AI step should answer four questions: How does the user know it may be wrong here? How can they see what the model relied on? How do they correct it in one step? And does the system learn from the correction? The interface elements behind these questions, such as source citations, confidence cues, one-click undo and correction entry points, are often treated as “later polish.” They should be designed together with the main feature.

The Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction compiled by Microsoft Research and collaborators offer 18 validated design guidelines, organized by four phases of interaction: initially, during interaction, when wrong, and over time. The authors tested them with 49 practitioners across 20 products.[3] What is worth borrowing is not any single item but the timeline-based way of checking: at first use, does the user have a reasonable expectation of what it can do? When it is wrong, is there a path to correction? After long use, is it actually adapting to the user?

3.3 Design “calibrated trust”

The goal is not to make users trust the AI, but to make their trust match the system’s real reliability. Over-trust lets errors through; under-trust leaves the feature unused. In practice there are three tools:

  • wherever something can be verified, give a verification entry point, for example cite the position in the source text so a click lets the user check;
  • when uncertain, say so; prefer a “needs human confirmation” flag over a fluent tone that hides the uncertainty;
  • make the boundary of the system’s ability visible in the interface, for example “currently handles these three document types.”

3.4 Friction is left in on purpose

In high-cost steps, one confirmation is not bad experience; it is an honest response to risk. What is truly bad is asking for confirmation in low-cost steps too: users quickly learn to click mechanically, and the confirmation loses its meaning. Keep friction exactly where it is needed.

04Prototype validation: climb one rung at a time, each answering one question

The costliest mistake in AI products is spending two months on a vertical slice that “runs,” only to find that users will never use it that way. The principle of prototype validation is: test the cheapest assumption first, using prototypes of different fidelity for different assumptions.

L4 Vertical sliceIs it stable end to end, and are cost and latency acceptable?Connect real data and tools, add evals, and cover only one scenarioRegression evals are stable; per-call cost and latency are within budget

Why the L2 Wizard of Oz suits AI products especially well

The Wizard of Oz method lets users believe they are interacting with a real system while a person responds behind the scenes. Nielsen Norman Group’s introduction to UX methods lists it as a way to study user behavior before the system is built.[4] For AI it has two extra benefits. First, you can validate “is this interaction worth building” without first solving “can the model do it,” separating product risk from technical risk. Second, the human responses naturally accumulate a batch of real inputs and examples of what a good answer looks like, which is exactly the seed of the eval set that comes after L3.

Why L2 and L3 can run in parallel

One looks at experience, the other at capability, and neither depends on the other. Combine the two conclusions and you land in one of four cases: good experience and enough capability, move on to L4; good experience but not enough capability, narrow the scope or add data; poor experience but enough capability, go back and rework the interaction; poor on both, stop. This is far cheaper than “build it first and see.”

Write down kill criteria

Before each rung begins, write down what result means stop and what result means continue. It is the simplest guard against sunk-cost thinking. A good kill criterion looks like this: “On 40 real samples, if fewer than half are judged acceptable by hand, and none of the top three failure causes can be fixed by changing the prompt, stop at L3.”

05Evals are the new PRD

A traditional requirements document describes expected behavior in words, and the product is accepted when the test cases pass. In an AI product, the eval set is the executable part of the requirements document: it defines the team’s shared understanding of “done right,” and every change can be checked against it.

In his article, Hamel Husain stresses that evals are central to iterating on an AI product, and that the pass rate needed to ship is itself a product decision, not a purely technical metric.[1] This is often overlooked: the threshold needs the product owner’s involvement, because it expresses how much failure you are willing to let users bear.

5.1 Start from real samples; read first, then define metrics

The most common mistake is to start by dreaming up a pile of generic metrics such as accuracy, relevance and fluency. A more effective order is: collect real (or high-fidelity synthetic) inputs, read every model output, record what each failure looks like, and then group the recurring failures.[1] Reading traces is tedious, but it is the step with the highest information density in the whole evaluation, because it tells you how your product will actually break.

5.2 Write checks as questions that can be answered true or false

A 1–5 “quality” score is hard to get two people to agree on. Break it into several checks that can be answered “yes” or “no,” and agreement improves a lot, and problems are easier to locate. There are two kinds of check:

  • Hard constraints: a violation is an incident and all of them must pass. For example, “must not invent clause numbers that do not exist in the source.” Use code assertions wherever possible;
  • Soft quality: affects how good the output is but is not fatal. For example, “does the summary cover the key conclusions?” This can go to a model judge, or be sampled by hand.

5.3 Let a model be the judge, but calibrate it first

Batch-grading with a model is cheap and fast, but the model itself makes mistakes and has preferences. A workable approach: have people judge a batch of samples first, then have the model judge the same batch, and compare how closely the two agree; where they differ a lot, go back and fix the judge’s prompt. Only a calibrated model judge deserves a place in the automated flow.[1] Even so, sample for human review periodically, so the judge does not drift together with the product.

5.4 Look at groups, not the overall average

A one-point rise in overall pass rate can hide a sharp drop in a key group. Group the eval set by scenario, input type and user segment, and compare group by group in regression. A change that makes things “better overall” but makes the most important group worse should not ship.

5.5 The eval set grows

Every failure found online should flow back into the eval set as a new sample. That way the set gets closer to the real world and to the actual boundary of the product. It is one of the most valuable data assets a team accumulates.

A line for product managers

You do not have to write the eval code yourself, but three things in an eval must be decided by you: what counts as right, how much is enough, and what must never go wrong.

06Cost, latency and reliability are product attributes

In traditional software, cost is a back-end matter. Every call to an AI feature spends money, latency becomes experience directly, and both trade off against quality: stronger models are more expensive and slower; multiple retries are steadier but slower. Anthropic’s article also states plainly that agentic systems usually trade higher latency and cost for better task performance, and that you must judge whether the trade is worth it.[2]

I prefer to measure by effective unit cost rather than the price of a single call:

Effective unit cost ≈ cost per call ÷ first-pass rate + human review costA framework for thinking, not an industry-standard formula

A low first-pass rate means retries or rework, so the cheap-per-call option can end up more expensive; the cost of human review is often underestimated, and once you count it, a “labor-saving” feature sometimes does not save. Conversely, this view also tells you where a stronger model is worth it and where a small model plus rules is enough.

Latency should be budgeted by scenario: in an interaction where the user stares and waits, a few seconds is the line between good and bad experience; background batch processing can trade it for a steadier approach. Write the budget into the requirements and accept it against real data at L4.

07Workflow or agent: start with the simplest

Anthropic distinguishes “workflows” from “agents”: in the former, predefined code paths orchestrate models and tools; in the latter, the model dynamically decides its own process and tool use. The article also summarizes several common workflow patterns, including prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, orchestrator-workers and evaluator-optimizer, and advises starting simple and adding complexity as needed.[2]

From a product angle I add two rules of thumb:

  • If the path can be written down, write it down. Use a workflow for tasks with a fixed process: behavior is predictable, evaluable and debuggable, and the user’s mental expectation is stable;
  • Consider an agent only when the number of steps cannot be known in advance and decisions must adapt to intermediate results, and set boundaries at the same time: which tools it may use, the maximum number of steps, and which actions need human confirmation. The autonomy dial (Fig. 3) applies directly here.

08A real example: where humans sit when building Keysuit with an AI assistant

Keysuit is a macOS menu-bar app I built that turns keystrokes into sound from selectable themes. Its development leans heavily on an AI coding assistant, so the project carries a set of working rules written for the assistant. The facts below come from the project’s own AGENTS.md and README. I will not discuss the product itself here, only how people and AI divide the work and how it is verified.

8.1 One milestone at a time, then stop

The project is split into four milestones: M1 sound, M2 themes and stats, M3 settings and events, M4 full experience. The rules require the assistant to advance only one at a time, stop and report when done, and wait for a human to confirm before continuing. This is equivalent to pinning autonomy at levels L2–L3: it may write on its own, but every delivery passes through a human checkpoint.

8.2 The report format is the acceptance checklist

Each report must state: what was done, how it was verified, the result of each checklist item (pass, doubtful, fail), where it deviates from the requirements, and which questions need a human decision. The “doubtful” level matters: it forces the assistant to say where it is not sure, instead of dressing up uncertainty as completion.

8.3 Write “must never go wrong” as hard constraints

The app’s privacy rule is: never read, record or print the characters that are pressed. Separately, the path from keystroke to sound may not go through a main-thread queue or a disk read, which is a hard constraint on latency. Neither is “do your best”; each is a red line that every change is checked against, corresponding to the hard constraints in an eval.

8.4 Some verification can only be done by a person

Choosing the signing identity, granting the input-monitoring permission, and judging latency and sound quality are explicitly assigned to a human; the last of these is done by ear and with slow-motion video. This is a plain but important fact: when the quality standard is sensory and subjective, the evaluation has to include a person. Pretending it can be fully automated only gets you a product that is self-consistent but not pleasant to use.

In addition, themes are designed as data (one description file per theme), and placeholder synthetic sounds are clearly marked. That way replacing assets requires no code change, and it is plain which are real assets and which are placeholders, a design that isolates the parts that change.

09Limitations and counterarguments

  • This is a working framework, not a law validated on a large sample. The research and articles cited provide grounding, but the division into stages, the five autonomy levels and the way I write effective unit cost are my own synthesis.
  • Small teams and early exploration need not copy every step. Often an afternoon at L3 is enough to drop a bad idea; an eval set is already valuable starting from twenty or thirty real samples, and need not wait for completeness.
  • Evals make you “optimize the eval.” Once a metric becomes the target, it gets gamed. Returning regularly to real usage data is the correction for that drift.
  • Models change fast. What needs a complex workflow today may take a single prompt tomorrow. So an eval set deserves long-term investment more than any specific prompt does.
  • Subjective quality is hard to quantify fully. For products such as writing, design and sound quality, a person with taste must still make the final call; evals only help you avoid going backward.

10Ten checks before kicking off

  • You can say in one sentence: who, in what scenario, how they do it now, and where it hurts.
  • You wrote a baseline that does not use AI, and you know how much of the problem it solves.
  • You placed the feature in the “cost × detectability” matrix and chose an autonomy level accordingly.
  • You listed the hard constraints: what must absolutely never happen.
  • Before each rung of prototyping, you wrote down the passing standard and the kill criteria.
  • You collected at least a few dozen real samples and read the model’s outputs yourself.
  • You grouped the failure modes and wrote binary checks for the main ones.
  • You know “what pass rate is enough to ship,” and the product owner made that call.
  • You estimated the effective unit cost and the latency budget, and wrote them into the requirements.
  • You designed the path after an error: noticing, evidence, correction, and flowing back into the eval set.
