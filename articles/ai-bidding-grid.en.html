Putting an LLM into Bidding:
A Product Teardown of a Power-Industry Bid Workbench

A bid document is not a writing contest. The real design focus is parsing, retrieval, constraints and checking, with a person always in the loop.

Author: ChrisAbout 18 min read4 figures · 1 table

ABSTRACT

This article takes apart an AI bid workbench for the power industry (the open-source repository ai_bidding_power_grid) and discusses:

  1. why it stops at “draft, reviewed by a person” rather than going fully automatic;
  2. the design trade-offs in its four stages: parsing, retrieval, drafting and compliance review;
  3. viewing it through the framework of the methodology article: which evals it lacks, and what to add next.

The facts come from the repository’s README and evaluation report; the product analysis is the author’s judgment.

01A bid document is not a writing contest

Asked to have a large model write a bid document, most people first think of “is the prose good enough?” Anyone who has bid knows that is not the hard part.

A bid document is hard because it must satisfy three things at once: it must respond to the tender requirements with nothing missed, since failing a substantive clause usually means the bid is disqualified outright; it must score against the evaluation method, with content and evidence for every scoring item; and its content must be true, with no invented qualifications, track record or specifications. None of the three is solved by “writing beautifully.”

This sets the design focus for this kind of product: not making the text-generation step as strong as possible, but chaining together parsing, retrieval, constraints and checking into an inspectable pipeline, with a person always in the loop. What I take apart below is the AI bid workbench for the power industry that I built. It targets power engineering, equipment supply and operations service companies, and puts the tender document, the company knowledge base and bid writing in one tool.[1]

The boundary of this article

The repository’s README states explicitly: AI output must be checked against the original tender documents and company materials; compliance checks are only an aid to preparing the bid, do not guarantee a win, and do not replace the bidder’s final review.[1] Every design below rests on that premise.

02Draw the boundary first: what it does and does not do

In the methodology article I said an AI product must first decide its autonomy level. The choice here is clear: stop at “draft, reviewed by a person”, rather than “fully automatic bid document.” There are three reasons, all from the business itself:

  • The cost of an error is high, and hard to notice. Missing one substantive requirement raises no error at generation time, yet may cost the whole bid its eligibility at evaluation. This is exactly the “high cost, hard to notice” quadrant from the methodology, which only suits draft plus confirm.
  • Responsibility cannot be transferred. The bidder is responsible for the bid’s content; the tool provides drafting aid, and the final signature and review must be a person’s.
  • Materials have boundaries. The README recommends first importing de-identified credential or product materials, and in real business using only company materials you have the right to process.[1]

So the value of this product is not “bidding for you,” but turning a tender document of dozens of pages into a checklist that can be verified, then compressing the manual labor of writing and checking.

03The overall flow: six steps, four human checkpoints

The first-use path recommended in the README is itself a pipeline with checkpoints: first fill in the company profile in system settings and import de-identified materials; upload the tender document and check the requirements, scoring items and risks that were parsed; generate the volume outline, choose sections to generate body text for, and edit in the editor; finally look at response coverage and AI usage records, then export DOCX.[1]

Note where the checkpoints sit: all of them are placed before the output of one step is “taken as true” by the next. If parsing is wrong, the outline, body text and compliance check that follow will all be wrong, so the first checkpoint is the most expensive and the most worth investing in.

04Parsing: turn a long document into a structured checklist

The output of the parsing step is the “source of truth” for every step after it. According to a description in an evaluation report inside the repository, the system extracts six kinds of information from the tender document: project overview, qualification requirements, commercial requirements, technical requirements, scoring items and risk items; for scanned files, images and complex tables, MinerU can be used for OCR.[2]

From a product angle, this step has two design points:

  • Structured, not a summary. A summary reads well but cannot be checked item by item; a list of “requirements, scoring items, risk items” can serve later as the baseline for the compliance check.
  • Let people correct quickly. Parsed results should be presented in a form easy to verify, and be editable. Scanned-file recognition needs MinerU to be configured separately, which is a real dependency and a limitation that has to be made clear in the docs.[1]

05Retrieval: company material is not just “found”

What a bid document can least afford to get wrong is the company’s own facts: qualifications, track record, product parameters, model numbers. These should come from the knowledge base, not from the model’s “memory.” The knowledge-base retrieval in the README uses three layers: vector recall, Rerank and keyword fallback.[1]

Why the third layer? An evaluation report in the repository, reviewing an early version, noted that vector-only retrieval loses domain terms and model numbers, and recommended adding keyword retrieval and re-ranking.[2] The judgment matches intuition: vectors are good at “similar in meaning,” not at “must match exactly.” Equipment model numbers, clause numbers and certificate numbers are exactly the latter. The capabilities now listed in the README already include re-ranking and keyword fallback, in the direction that review recommended.

Product judgment

The goal of retrieval is not “finding related content” but “finding facts that can be cited.” So material entering the drafting prompt should carry its source, letting a person go back to the original to check during review.

06Drafting: constrain the model with rules

The evaluation report lists the “rule-based writing plan” as one of this system’s highlights: for each section the system computes a target word count and suggested page count, and decides whether a table, picture, qualification or case needs to be inserted, constraining the model’s output with rules rather than leaving it entirely to free play.[2]

Behind this is a general method: a constraint that can be written as a rule should not be left to the model to honor on its own. Length, structure and the elements that must appear are deterministic requirements, planned in code; the model is only responsible for writing the content fluently and in line with the material within that frame.

How long sections are written

The report notes that for core sections over 3,500 characters the system generates in multiple passes to avoid output being cut off by length limits, and body text is returned as a stream, so the user need not wait for a whole section before seeing content.[2]

A known risk: context pollution

The evaluation report also points out that the early version fed earlier sections to the model without trimming, so long generation could suffer context pollution, wasted tokens and diluted attention, and recommended compressing earlier text with summaries or a memory mechanism.[2] This risk is typical of long-text products: the longer the document, the more actively you must manage “what the model should see right now.”

07Compliance review: from “is it there” to “is it right”

Compliance review is the part of this product closest to risk. The evaluation report’s assessment of the early version was that the compliance check relied mainly on text matching, could only do a coarse comparison and struggled to judge whether the draft truly responds semantically; it recommended upgrading to LLM-based semantic cross-checking.[2] The README now describes it as “rule checks plus LLM semantic review, locating missing responses and giving remediation advice.”[1]

The two layers have different jobs. Rule checks are deterministic: does a key item in the requirements appear in the body text? Semantic review answers the harder question: it is mentioned in the body, but does it actually satisfy the requirement? With only the first layer, “mentioned” is mistaken for “responded”; with only the second, results are hard to reproduce.

08Cost and tracking: put the ledger in the product

A bid document is a typical case of generating long text in bulk: dozens of sections, each possibly generated in several passes, plus retrieval and review. The README lists “task records and records of model-call usage and cost” as part of delivery and tracking.[1]

This is not a nice extra. Without usage and cost records, you cannot answer “what does one bid document really cost,” and so cannot decide how to price it, whether to switch models, or whether caching is worth it. I analyze this part in “The Pricing and Cost Ledger of AI Products.”

09Through the lens of evals: what is still missing

This is the most honest section about this project. The README gives no quantified performance metrics or benchmark results; the evaluation report is only a qualitative assessment based on a code walkthrough, likewise with no benchmarks or test data.[1][2] In other words: it has a process design, but not yet an eval set that can be run repeatedly. 10Limitations and risks

  • No quantified metrics. At present there is no number to say “how good it is”; that is what the previous section is about adding.
  • Confidentiality and privacy. Tender and company materials often involve trade secrets. The evaluation report itself does not discuss security, privacy or confidentiality, which is an obvious gap; the README’s advice to use de-identified materials and to replace the example secrets on deployment is only a starting point.[1][2]
  • Dependence on external services. By default DeepSeek is used for writing and parsing, and the knowledge base depends on DashScope for embedding and reranking; the corresponding keys must both be configured, and the availability and cost of those services will affect the product.[1]
  • Not a guarantee of winning. The result of a bid depends on many factors outside the tool. This product can reduce omissions and manual labor, but cannot replace judgment about the project itself.
  • Illustrative content. The coverage matrix in Figure 4 and the checks in Table 1 are meant to show the method and do not represent real data.

11If I rebuilt it, what I would do first

  1. Collect dozens of de-identified real tender documents, label the scoring items and substantive requirements by hand, and use them as the first version of the eval set.
  2. Turn “does parsing cover all scoring items” into a regression check that runs on every change.
  3. Label every fact in the knowledge base with its source, and make the drafting prompt cite sources.
  4. Set a “better to over-report than to miss” threshold for compliance review, and have the business side make that call.
  5. Record usage and cost for every step from day one, and use them to work back to pricing.
