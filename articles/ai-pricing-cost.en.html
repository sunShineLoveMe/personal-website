The Pricing and Cost Ledger of AI Products

An AI feature spends money every time it is used. Work out the real cost first, then decide between subscription, usage-based and hybrid.

Author: ChrisAbout 15 min read4 figures · 2 tables · 1 checklist

ABSTRACT

Unlike traditional software, an AI feature’s marginal cost is not zero, and it varies enormously between users. This article proposes:

  1. counting cost by “effective unit cost” instead of the sticker price;
  2. cutting cost first with caching, batching, model tiering and context trimming, while guarding quality with an eval set;
  3. choosing a pricing model with three questions: does cost vary with usage, does the bill need to be predictable, can value be measured;
  4. using cost for the floor and value for the price, and recomputing regularly.

01First ask: who pays for every click

Traditional software has a reassuring property: serving one more user costs close to nothing at the margin. Pricing can therefore be almost entirely about value, and cost is a back-end matter.

AI features break this. Every call consumes inference resources; the more it is used, the more it costs, and the gap between users can be very large. In its guide for founders, Stripe calls this problem margin compression: when inference costs grow faster than revenue, a customer who pays a flat price but runs far more usage than expected is subsidized by customers who use less.[1]

One industry article cites a case: after a team launched an AI extraction feature on a flat subscription, 8% of customers were consuming 61% of the inference cost.[4] That is the company’s own account, not a universal number, but the shape it describes is typical: usage distributions are often long-tailed, and a fixed price offers no protection against the tail.

The stance of this article

Pricing is not “pick a number.” First work out the cost floor, then decide how to turn value into price. Cost decides how low you cannot go; value decides how high you can go.

02The cost ledger: the sticker price is not your real cost

In “Product Method for the AI Era” I introduced “effective unit cost.” Here I expand it into a ledger. The per-call price on the invoice is only the starting point of the real cost:

  • Retries and rework. A first-pass rate below 100% means that for each acceptable result you pay for more than one call on average. Take a 70% first-pass rate: cost is amplified about 1.43 times, so a “cheap small model” is not necessarily cheap.
  • Human review. If the product’s autonomy level is “draft, reviewed by a person,” every output needs someone to look at it. A person’s time is usually far more expensive than tokens, and once counted it may well become the largest item. The figures in the chart are illustrative values chosen to show the structure; substitute your own data.
  • The hidden part. Running costs for retrieval, vector storage, document parsing, monitoring and evals also rise with usage, but are rarely counted in “cost per call.”
Effective unit cost ≈ cost per call ÷ first-pass rate + human review cost + allocated infrastructure costA framework for thinking, not an industry-standard formula

Work back from the cost floor to a minimum price

Freemius suggests first finding the cost floor, then working back from a target gross margin to a price: the price must be at least the effective unit cost divided by (1 minus the target gross margin).[4] For example, if the effective unit cost is 1 yuan and the target gross margin is 60%, the unit price should not be below 2.5 yuan. The arithmetic is simple, but it pulls discussions such as “should we have a free tier” or “should we include unlimited usage” back to concrete numbers.

03Cut cost before touching price: four levers

Before changing pricing, there are usually cheaper ways to make the cost ledger look better. I order the four levers below from least to most impact on product experience.

LeverWhat to doSuited forCosts and cautions
Prompt cachingCache long prefixes that recur (system prompt, fixed reference material); on a hit, the read price is far below normal input[3]The same long context used many timesWriting to the cache costs slightly more than normal input; caches expire; the prefix must be identical to hit
Batch processingSubmit asynchronously; the official docs give a 50% discount on both input and output, with a processing window of up to 24 hours[2]Non-real-time bulk jobs, such as offline parsing or bulk taggingNot for interactions that need instant feedback; can be stacked with caching
Model tieringSmall models for simple tasks, big models only for hard ones; route first, then executeProducts whose task difficulty varies widelyYou need evals to prove the small model is enough, otherwise it is only a gamble
Shorter context and outputCarry only the necessary material, constrain output length and formatAlmost every productOver-trimming can hurt quality; let the eval set decide

Table 1Four cost-cutting levers. Discount multipliers and conditions are as per the official pricing pages, and change with model and time.

It is worth stressing: any cost-cutting measure must pass a regression check against the eval set. If cost falls and the pass rate falls too, the effective unit cost can actually rise. That is why I put evals ahead of pricing.

04Subscription, usage, hybrid, outcome: what each one bets on

Stripe’s guide groups the common AI pricing structures into tiered subscription, usage-based billing, a hybrid of subscription plus usage, per-seat, outcome-based and others, and notes that most AI products use one of them or a combination of two.[1] I prefer to understand them by “what each one is betting on.”

Figure 3 is the shortest path to understanding the risk of subscriptions: subscription revenue is a flat line, while cost rises with usage. To the left of the crossing point, light users contribute a surplus; to the right, every extra bit of usage adds to the loss, and those losses are covered by the light users’ surplus.

05How to choose: three questions

  1. Does cost vary a lot with usage? If not, use the simplest tiered subscription; the strength of subscription is that users understand it, so do not complicate it just to be “more scientific.”
  2. Do users need a predictable bill? For enterprises or customers with budget approval processes, they usually do. A hybrid model often fits better here: the base subscription gives predictability, while quota and overage protect margin.
  3. Can the value be measured, and is attribution clear? Only when you are confident on this point is outcome-based pricing worth considering; otherwise it makes disputes part of the product.

After choosing a model, I have two more bottom lines of my own. First, let users see their usage before they spend; a usage panel is not a nice extra but part of trust design. Second, always keep an exit that lets you change the price, because models and costs change faster than contracts.

06Costs are falling; prices should not fall with them

One compiled industry statistics page notes that the cost of inference at GPT-3.5 level fell roughly 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024.[5] A compilation page like this is second-hand, and the basis of its numbers needs checking yourself, but the direction is hardly disputed: the unit cost of inference keeps falling.

This sets a trap for “cost-plus” pricing. If your price is anchored to cost, then when cost falls, revenue falls passively with it; and once a competitor cuts prices, it is hard to explain why you do not. A steadier way of thinking:

  • Use cost for the floor, value for the price. Cost tells you how low you cannot go; why users are willing to pay is set by value;
  • Spend the dividend of falling costs on better experience, not only on lower prices. For example a higher pass rate, faster responses, more features;
  • Recompute the cost ledger regularly. After each model swap, prompt change or caching-strategy adjustment, update the effective unit cost.

07The ledger in two real projects

The bid workbench: making cost a visible feature

The power-industry bid workbench I built lists “task records and records of model-call usage and cost” in its README as part of its delivery and tracking capabilities.[7] For a product that generates lots of long text per project, this is necessary: without usage records you cannot know the real cost of one bid document, and so cannot price it. The details of this project are in the other article, “Putting an LLM into Bidding.”

Keysuit: a completely different cost structure

Keysuit is a macOS app that runs in a sandbox and does not use the network; the project rules explicitly require that it not use the network.[6] That means it has no pay-per-call model inference cost, and its marginal cost structure differs from everything discussed above; its pricing should revolve around value and distribution cost, not an inference bill. It is currently in a free public beta, and the price of the stable version is not yet set, a typical “validate value first, set price later” stage.

08Limitations

  • Illustrative numbers are not benchmarks. The relative values in Figure 1 and the cost-floor worked example only show structure; please replace them with your own data.
  • Prices and discounts change. The caching multiplier, the batch discount and each model’s price should be taken from the official pricing page on the day; I cite the values I could verify at the time of writing.
  • Some sources are second-hand. The cases and statistics in industry articles may not share a basis or sample; they are only suitable for understanding direction, not as direct evidence.
  • Pricing is more than a cost question. Brand, competition and customers’ payment habits all change the final decision; this article covers only the part most related to cost.

09Ten self-checks before pricing

  • You know the effective unit cost of one acceptable result, not only the price of a single call.
  • You have real data on first-pass rate, number of retries and human review time.
  • You have tried caching, batching, model tiering and context trimming, and confirmed with the eval set that quality did not drop.
  • You have drawn the “subscription vs. usage” crossing point: you know which tier of users starts losing you money.
  • There are clear quotas or rate limits; you have not promised “unlimited usage.”
  • Users can see their own usage before they spend.
  • You have worked back from the target gross margin to a minimum price.
  • The price is anchored to value; cost is only the floor.
  • You recompute the cost ledger after every model swap or prompt change.
  • Price and quota rules can be adjusted without overturning contracts.
